# Exosphere Newsletter — 2026-10-06

## Highlights

### LHS 1140b: First Confirmed Atmosphere on a Habitable-Zone Rocky Planet
In a landmark discovery, astronomers have detected helium escaping from LHS 1140b, a rocky planet about 49 light-years away in the habitable zone of its star. This marks the first confirmed detection of an atmosphere on a potentially habitable rocky planet, with observations indicating that the planet's atmosphere has persisted for at least 3.1 billion years despite stellar bombardment. The detection of escaping helium signatures suggests a durable secondary atmosphere that could support life.

### K2-18b: Strongest Evidence Yet of Potential Biosignatures
Cambridge scientists have detected dimethyl sulfide (DMS) and dimethyl disulfide (DMDS) in the atmosphere of K2-18b, a Hycean world with liquid water and habitable temperatures. These organic molecules are produced on Earth exclusively by living organisms, particularly marine phytoplankton. While scientists remain cautiously optimistic, the detection represents potentially the strongest evidence yet for extraterrestrial biosignatures, complementing earlier JWST discoveries of methane and carbon dioxide in K2-18b's atmosphere.

### JWST Completes Rocky Worlds Program: New Era in Exoplanet Characterization
The James Webb Space Telescope has completed its coordinated observations of rocky exoplanets, including detailed surface composition studies of LHS 3844 b and atmosphere detection on rocky worlds. The data reveals these planets often have basalt-like surfaces resembling Mercury, with implications for understanding planetary geology and atmospheric retention on worlds without substantial magnetic fields.

### HD 156295 b: Planet in Habitable Zone of Hottest Star to Host One
Researchers have identified a potential planet candidate within the habitable zone of HD 156295, a star approximately 2,000°C hotter than our Sun. If confirmed, HD 156295 b would represent the hottest star known to potentially host a habitable-zone planet, expanding our understanding of where life-supporting worlds can exist and the range of stellar environments compatible with habitability.

### White Dwarf Planet Accretion Candidate Discovered
A second-generation planet candidate has been detected accreting onto a white dwarf, published in Nature Astronomy. This unusual system provides insights into planetary dynamics around evolved stars and may represent a new class of observable planetary systems at late stages of stellar evolution.

## Speculative Corner

The discoveries of today point toward an exciting convergence: we are entering an era where we can not only detect atmospheres on distant rocky worlds but also characterize their chemical composition with unprecedented detail. The detection of helium escape from LHS 1140b is particularly intriguing because it suggests that rocky planets—once thought to rapidly lose their atmospheres—can retain substantial gas envelopes over billions of years. This reframes our understanding of planetary habitability: a rocky world with a billion-year-old atmosphere is fundamentally different from a world that lost its atmosphere in the first few hundred million years.

The case of K2-18b raises even more tantalizing questions about biosignature detection. While DMS and DMDS are produced by terrestrial life, these molecules could theoretically arise through non-biological pathways on alien worlds with different chemistry. However, the convergence of multiple biosignature candidates—organic molecules detected by JWST, now complemented by DMS/DMDS signatures—suggests we may be witnessing our first genuine detection of extraterrestrial life. If confirmed through follow-up observations, K2-18b would represent a paradigm shift: not merely discovering that habitable planets exist, but finding evidence that they are actually *inhabited*.

The implications extend beyond K2-18b. The successful characterization of rocky worlds' surfaces and atmospheres via JWST now provides a roadmap for systematic surveys of potentially habitable exoplanets. As the Habitable Worlds Observatory and future instruments come online, we stand poised to conduct the largest survey of potentially life-bearing worlds ever attempted. The next decade may finally answer whether the chemical complexity that led to life on Earth is unique to our world, or whether the universe is teeming with biosignatures waiting to be discovered.
