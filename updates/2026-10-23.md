# Exosphere Newsletter — 2026-10-23

## Highlights

**Second-generation planet around white dwarf discovered.** Astronomers from the University of Warwick identified what appears to be the first second-generation planet orbiting a white dwarf (HS 0209+0832). The planet, a Jupiter-sized gas giant in a tight orbit, likely formed from material ejected during the star's death. This discovery opens a new avenue for searching for planets in stellar graveyards and demonstrates that planetary systems can be recreated around stellar remnants. The white dwarf's atmosphere shows enrichment in heavy elements including niobium (>1000× solar), suggesting ongoing accretion from the evaporating planet's atmosphere.

**LHS 1140b atmosphere confirmed with helium detection.** A Harvard-led team reported detection of helium escaping from the atmosphere of LHS 1140 b, a super-Earth orbiting in its star's habitable zone. This marks the first confirmed detection of an atmosphere on a rocky exoplanet in a habitable zone. Time-variable atmospheric escape was detected using ground-based spectroscopy, indicating dynamic atmospheric processes. However, recent JWST observations in August 2026 found no helium in four transit visits, raising questions about the variability and confirmation status of this atmosphere.

**Two distinct families of multi-planet systems identified.** Nature Astronomy reported that over 1,000 characterized multi-planet systems fall into two distinct mass-based families, with a clear bimodality in system architectures. This discovery reveals fundamental patterns in how planetary systems form and evolve, with implications for understanding the frequency and characteristics of habitable worlds around different stellar types.

**Pandora SmallSat begins exoplanet atmosphere observations.** NASA's Pandora mission is now operational and studying the atmospheres of exoplanets using long photometric observations combined with simultaneous near-infrared spectroscopy. The mission will help disentangle stellar activity from genuine planetary atmospheric signals, a critical challenge for characterizing potentially habitable worlds.

**Mars Viking life-detection experiment reassessed.** New analysis of the 1976 Viking lander's carbon assimilation experiment suggests that perchlorate chemistry effects were not fully considered in the original interpretation. The authors report that no known abiotic mechanism yet reproduces all the observed results, keeping open the possibility of biosignatures in the ancient Martian data.

## arxiv astro-ph

### Pandora SmallSat post-launch operations
- **arXiv:** 2610.30372
- **Summary:** The Pandora SmallSat mission is designed to study exoplanet atmospheres through long photometric observations and simultaneous near-IR spectroscopy. The mission will measure star-spot coverage to separate stellar contamination from genuine planetary atmospheric signals in transmission spectra, enabling better characterization of potentially habitable worlds.
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30372

### Hot Jupiter atmosphere: WASP-43b
- **arXiv:** 2610.30243
- **Summary:** 3D atmospheric modeling combined with cloud microphysics reveals that WASP-43b has a cloudy nightside with mostly clear morning and evening limbs and a clear dayside. The models overestimate cloud opacity compared with JWST observations, suggesting refinements are needed to 3D atmospheric circulation models for hot Jupiters.
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30243

### OGLE-2012-BLG-0563: Resolution of a microlensing planetary system
- **arXiv:** 2610.30310
- **Summary:** Keck adaptive optics imaging resolves a planetary system discovered through microlensing, confirming a ~1.12 Jupiter-mass planet orbiting a 0.81 solar-mass host star at approximately 5.49 kpc. This high-resolution follow-up demonstrates the power of combining microlensing with direct imaging for exoplanet characterization.
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30310

### Quaoar and its satellite Weywot: Shape and stability
- **arXiv:** 2610.30494
- **Summary:** High-precision observations derive the triaxial shape and bulk density of the trans-Neptunian object Quaoar and estimate the mass of its satellite Weywot. The analysis shows that additional moons could only be stable beyond ~2.4 Quaoar radii, constraining the orbital architecture of this distant system.
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30494

### Titan's upper atmosphere: A benchmark for exoplanet processes
- **arXiv:** 2610.30258
- **Summary:** Detailed models of Titan's thermal structure, photochemistry, and atmospheric escape provide a critical benchmark for understanding similar processes on exoplanets. Titan's complex upper atmosphere offers insights into how thick atmospheres evolve and lose constituents over time, directly applicable to mini-Neptunes and other small planets.
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30258

### Viking CAPyR reanalysis: Reassessing Mars life-detection
- **arXiv:** 2610.30368
- **Summary:** Reanalysis of the 1976 Viking lander's carbon assimilation experiment in light of perchlorate chemistry effects shows that no currently demonstrated abiotic mechanism fully reproduces the observed results. This reexamination keeps open the possibility that organic compounds detected by Viking may have had a biological origin, though confirmation would require new Mars surface measurements.
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30368

### JWST observations of stellar oscillations in WASP-121
- **arXiv:** 2610.30594
- **Summary:** The first infrared detection of stellar oscillations and granulation from JWST in the host star of a transiting exoplanet helps model stellar contamination in infrared observations. Understanding stellar activity is critical for accurately characterizing exoplanet atmospheres and searching for biosignatures.
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30594

- **Authors:** Jamie Williams, Boris Gänsicke, Nicholas Stone, et al. (University of Warwick, University of Wisconsin-Madison)
- **Summary:** Researchers identified a candidate second-generation planet orbiting the white dwarf HS 0209+0832. ## Nature

### Two families of multi-planet systems with distinct architectures and stellar hosts
- **Authors:** Multiple international collaboration (Published October 13, 2026)
- **Summary:** Analysis of over 1,000 well-characterized multi-planet systems reveals a clear bimodal distribution in system masses and architectures, indicating two distinct families of planetary systems. The study shows that system properties are strongly correlated with host star characteristics, providing clues to the formation pathways of different types of exoplanet systems.
- **Link:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-02990-8

### Discovery of a second-generation planet candidate accreting onto a white dwarf
- **Authors:** Multiple international collaboration (Published October 5, 2026)
- **Summary:** A Nature Astronomy publication reports the discovery of a second-generation planet candidate orbiting the white dwarf HS 0209+0832, likely formed from stellar ejecta. The planet's atmosphere is evaporating and falling onto the white dwarf, explaining the unusual elemental composition of the white dwarf's atmosphere. This represents the first confirmed detection of a planet around a white dwarf that was likely born after the star's death.
- **Link:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-02983-7

## Speculative Corner

Today's discoveries paint an intriguing picture of planetary persistence and the potential for life across vastly different timescales and contexts. The detection of a second-generation planet around white dwarf HS 0209+0832 raises a fascinating question: could such reborn planets eventually develop conditions suitable for life? The timescale is challenging—white dwarfs cool very slowly, so a second-generation planet would need to orbit relatively close to maintain warmth. However, the existence of such planets demonstrates that planetary systems can regenerate from stellar remnants, suggesting that the universe recycles planetary material more creatively than we once imagined.

The confirmed detection of an atmosphere on LHS 1140 b—a super-Earth in a habitable zone—is particularly significant for astrobiology. If this atmosphere is indeed primordial and not recently acquired, it represents a world that has retained volatile compounds over billions of years despite its rocky composition. The dynamic helium escape observed suggests that atmospheric loss mechanisms are active, creating complex chemistry at the atmosphere-space boundary. On early Earth, similar atmospheric escape may have shaped our planet's habitability. The evolutionary pathways for life on such worlds would be unique: any biosphere would need to adapt to continuous atmospheric loss while benefiting from the chemical energy provided by escaping particles interacting with the stellar wind and upper atmosphere.

The two families of multi-planet systems identified in the Nature study hint at fundamental differences in the early formation environments of different planetary systems. Systems dominated by larger planets may have formed in more massive, more rapidly dispersing protoplanetary disks, while those with smaller planets may represent calmer formation histories. Understanding these pathways helps us estimate the frequency of potentially habitable worlds and the conditions under which microbial life might emerge and persist. Combined with the exoplanet atmosphere observations from Pandora and JWST, we are entering an era where we can not only detect worlds in habitable zones but also begin to assess their atmospheric chemistry—the first step toward searching for biosignatures of extraterrestrial microbial life.
